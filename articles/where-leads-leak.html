Where leads leak

Six silent failures between the ad and the agent.

29 November 2026 · Where leads leak · 5 min read

Every lead costs money twice. Once when you pay for the ad, and again when it disappears quietly somewhere between the ad and the agent.

When that happens, everyone has a story. Sales says the leads are bad. Marketing says they sent them. In my experience, both are partly right. The leads were real, and they were sent. They just never arrived where they were supposed to.

I'm a developer at a real-estate company in Dubai, and a big part of my job is the pipe that carries leads from the ads to the people who call them. Over the last few months I found six places where that pipe was leaking. What surprised me wasn't that they existed. It was that none of them ever threw an error. Every one of them said "OK" somewhere.

1. The form that wasn't a lead

Our website had a lot of forms. Some of them created a lead in the CRM. Others only sent an email. From the outside they looked exactly the same. Someone filled in their details, pressed submit and saw a thank-you message.

But only some of those people became leads. The rest were an email in an inbox, and an email in an inbox doesn't get a call.

The fix was a simple rule. Every form on the site is a lead, except the careers form. Then I went back and pulled in the people we'd missed.

2. The button tap nobody passed on

We send WhatsApp messages with quick-reply buttons, so people can say they're interested with one tap. It's the easiest thing in the world for the person on the other end.

The problem was that the WhatsApp tool we use never passed those taps on to us. The person tapped, saw their tap go through, and as far as they knew, they'd replied. On our side, nothing happened. They never became a lead, so nobody called them.

The fix was to read the taps directly instead of waiting to be told about them, and then to go back and recover everyone who had tapped and been missed.

3. The phone number that lost its country code

Leads from ad forms were arriving with phone numbers that couldn't be dialled. The number was there and it looked like a number, but the country code had been lost somewhere on the way in.

Imagine filling in a form, waiting for a call, and never getting one because of a missing country code.

Now every number is put into the same format the moment it comes in, with the country code checked. The ones that were already broken got fixed too.

4. The CRM that said OK

This is my favourite one, if you can have a favourite failure.

When you send data to another system and it replies "200 OK", it's easy to think the job is done. All it really means is that the other system heard you. Our CRM accepted every lead we sent and replied OK every time. And quietly, it dropped the phone number from every one of them, because it expected the number in a slightly different shape.

Nothing crashed. Nothing looked wrong in any log. The leads were all there, with names and projects. You just couldn't call any of them.

So now it's a rule for everything I build. After every write to another system, read the record back and compare it with what you sent. If they don't match, that's an error, whatever the other system says.

5. The lead parked with a holding user

When a lead comes in, the router doesn't always know straight away who should get it. So it gives the lead to a holding account while it works that out.

But there was a gap in the rules, and some leads that were parked there never got picked up again. They weren't lost exactly. They were in the CRM, assigned to an account that nobody works from. To everyone looking at their own list, those leads didn't exist.

Now nothing stays parked without a timer. If a lead sits with the holding account for too long, it gets moved on, and I get an alert so I can see why it got stuck in the first place.

6. The burst that took the door off

This one didn't drop leads one at a time. It knocked over the door they come in through.

When the team sends a big WhatsApp broadcast, every message that gets delivered sends a receipt back to us. A big broadcast means a flood of receipts, all at once. Every one of them woke up the whole app, as if it were a new message from a customer. So the intake got overwhelmed, and for a while, real messages from real people couldn't get in.

The fix was to stop treating every knock on the door the same way. Receipts now go into a fast queue and get dealt with in the background. A message from an actual person skips the queue.

How to check your own pipe

None of these needed anything clever to fix. They needed someone to notice. If you look after leads anywhere, these are the checks I'd start with:

  • Count your leads at every step, from the ad platform to the intake, to the CRM, to the agent. Compare the counts every day. A gap between two steps is a leak.
  • After every write to another system, read the record back.
  • Get an alert when the intake goes quiet. Silence is a symptom.
  • Give every failure you fix an ID, a rule and a test, so it can't come back in a different shape.
  • Keep the original form or message next to the lead, so you can always see what the person actually sent.

Looking at both ends

When I look back at all six, none of them needed AI to find them. They needed someone to look at both ends of the pipe at the same time, the ad on one side and the agent on the other, and ask why the two didn't match.

What AI changed is how cheap that question is. Checking every step used to be a job nobody had time for. Now I can ask it in a minute, so I ask it a lot more often.
